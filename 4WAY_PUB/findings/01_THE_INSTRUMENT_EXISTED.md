# Finding 1 — the instrument existed

**1940.** J.R. Squire established that the difference in transmission of red and
infrared light permits oxygen saturation to be computed.

**1940–1941.** Glenn Allan Millikan built the first practical portable oximeter:
an earpiece holding an incandescent bulb, red and green filters, and a selenium
barrier-layer photocell. Built 1940, presented to the American Physiological
Society 1941. Commissioned at Lord Adrian's request for the Royal Air Force,
because pilots were losing consciousness in high-altitude dogfights. He coined
the word *oximeter*.

**1972–1974.** Takuo Aoyagi derived pulse oximetry from the ratio of red to
infrared absorption — the modern method.

**1986.** The American Society of Anesthesiologists made pulse oximetry
mandatory for every administration of anesthesia. Standard II § 2.1: *"a
quantitative method of assessing oxygenation such as pulse oximetry shall be
employed."* Approved 21 October 1986, in force continuously since.

## The counting was never the hard part

A human heart does not exceed roughly 300 beats per minute. Allow 500. That is
**8.33 events per second**. Everything a pulse or breathing monitor must do
reduces to counting that, and recording when.

| Device | Era | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary wristwatch balance wheel | standard before 1970 | **5 beats/sec** (18,000 BPH) |
| Elgin "Jitterbug" military stopwatch | World War II | **40 beats/sec** (144,000 BPH) |
| Wynn-Williams thyratron counter | published 1931 | particle counting "at much higher rates than previously" |
| Wynn-Williams scale-of-two counter | published 1932 | copied into physics laboratories across Europe and the US |

A wristwatch of the period already ticked five times a second — the rate a racing
heart needs. The wartime Elgin ran at forty, worn on a wrist, mass-produced.
Electronic counters existed for nuclear physics **nine years before Millikan
built the oximeter**, and were standard laboratory equipment by the time he did.

## The comparison

The second is defined as 9,192,631,770 oscillations of caesium-133. Every one of
those is counted.

| What is counted | Events per second |
|---|---|
| To define one second | 9,192,631,770 |
| To log a human heartbeat at 500/min | 8.33 |
| Every apnea in every sleeping human, sourced estimate | 1,231,199 |

A factor of **1.1 billion** against a single heartbeat. Against every apnea on
Earth at once, still **7,467 : 1**.

We built the harder instrument, shrank it to a chip, put it in orbit, and rebuilt
the definition of time around it. We did not require a device measuring a
pregnant woman's oxygen to state how it averaged the number.

## A measurement interval is a declaration, not a discovery

No interval defines itself. 9,192,631,770 was chosen by a committee in 1967 to
match the second already in use — which came from the day. A real period, a
chosen size, written down so that everyone measuring would mean the same thing.

Every working interval standard exists because somebody stated it.

Nobody ever stated one for breath.

## What this does not establish

That a 1940s device was accurate enough for obstetric screening, or that anyone
then knew what to look for. The claim is availability, not clinical readiness.
A 1940 earpiece oximeter is not a 2026 recording device; the continuity runs from
principle to present, not device to device.
