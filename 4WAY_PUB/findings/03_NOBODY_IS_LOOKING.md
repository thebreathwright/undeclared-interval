# Finding 3 — nobody is looking

## The carve-out

United States Preventive Services Task Force, *Obstructive Sleep Apnea in
Adults: Screening*. Issued 15 November 2022. **Grade I.**

> The USPSTF concludes that the current evidence is insufficient to assess the
> balance of benefits and harms of screening for obstructive sleep apnea in the
> general adult population.

And then, verbatim:

> This recommendation does not apply to children, adolescents, or pregnant persons.

The body that decides what Americans are screened for did not conclude that
screening pregnant women is unsupported. It removed them from the population the
question was asked about. Pregnant women are not told the evidence is uncertain.
They are outside the sentence.

## The tool does not work

*BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth* 2024, DOI 10.1186/s12884-024-06753-z. Ninety-six
third-trimester women underwent polysomnography and completed the Berlin
Questionnaire; 81 bedpartners completed it about them.

**Sleep-disordered breathing was present in 43.8% of them.**

| Instrument | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| Berlin Questionnaire, RDI ≥ 5 | 83% | 37% |
| Wilson Optimized Model, RDI ≥ 5 | 45% | 83% |

> Screening and diagnosis of SDB during pregnancy remains a challenge, with
> existing screening tools underperforming during pregnancy.

> More work is needed to develop a pregnancy-specific tool for quick and accurate
> screening for SDB.

Bedpartner input did not help: AUC 0.69 against 0.73 for the women themselves.

## It is not new, and it is not only pregnancy

Lastra AC, Attarian HP. *Gender and the Genome* 2018;2(2):43–48.

- Clinical populations ran **8:1 male to female**. Epidemiology says **2:1**.
  The disease is not 8:1; the clinic is. Roughly three of four women with it
  never reached the room.
- **1997: 93% of women with moderate-to-severe OSA were undiagnosed.**
- It was not presentation. Women with the classic symptoms — loud snoring,
  daytime sleepiness — were diagnosed **14%** of the time against **25%** for
  men, and treated **11%** against **17%**.

## The threshold undercounts women by rule

Medicare requires a **4%** oxygen desaturation to score a hypopnea. The AASM uses
**3%**. Noridian's own clinician letter (rev. November 2024) states both and
gives no reason for the 4%.

Via Singh 2025's scoping review (*Sleep Sci Pract* 9(1):29):

- **48.9% of women would be undiagnosed under 4% criteria, against 28.5% of men**
  (Khalid).
- AHI rises **83% in women** and **64% in men** under 3% scoring (Won).
  **One remove — read Won at source before relying on this.**

So the scoring rule itself, before any clinic or referral, removes nearly twice
as many women as men from the count.

## What has no number at all

| | Count | Who counts it |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertension | **1.4 billion** adults 30–79 | WHO fact sheet, national surveillance, 44% diagnosed and treated |
| Sleep apnea | **936 million** adults 30–69 | one literature-based estimate, 2019 |
| Mouth breathing | **no figure, anywhere** | nobody |

What exists is children, in one country. Brazil, 419 schoolchildren aged 6–11:
**56.8%**. Across Brazilian cities, 26–56%. The review's own sentence: *"This
review highlighted the lack of studies on the prevalence of MB, specially in
adults."*

A condition measured at 26–56% in children cannot plausibly be rarer in adults
than one affecting 1.4 billion people. **The question has not been asked at
population scale.**

## Where the data that exists came from

Not from the institutions with the resources. The prevalence work on mouth
breathing came from Brazilian school studies. Nine US medical schools — drawn
from the BRIMR top-20 NIH-funded list — have held George Catlin's *Shut Your
Mouth and Save Your Life* in their medical collections since 1861, and produced
no prevalence data on the subject.

**Six of those nine copies are in vaults, rare-book rooms, special collections
or offsite storage: request-only.** You cannot walk past them. You must already
know the title to ask for it. The book did not hide. It was filed.

The shelf-time argument is not made here, and should not be: a book in a library
is not a reader. What is claimed is narrower and documented — the material was
also in Board of Education syllabuses (1904, 1909) requiring daily nasal-breathing
instruction from school entry, in Holt's standard 1897 pediatric text (childhood
mouth breathing and nocturnal respiratory pauses, pp. 33 and 264), and in clinical
practice (Lermoyez 1904). Curriculum, textbook and clinic — not one overlooked volume.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here identifies anyone's intent. The absence of a prevalence figure is
documented; why it is absent is not. A mechanism that produces this outcome
without anyone steering is available and should be stated: conditions get counted
when they acquire a category, a threshold and a billing code. Hypertension got a
cuff, a number and reimbursement. Mouth breathing never got a category, so it
never got a denominator.

The one question that is both answerable and unanswered: **who set 4%, on what
evidence, and what happened when the sex disparity became known.** That has a
paper trail — CMS rulemaking, comment periods, AASM position papers. It has not
been pulled here.
